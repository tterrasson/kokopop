The red ON AIR sign flickered on at midnight, and the studio went quiet except for the hum of the transmitter.
I adjusted the microphone, checked the levels, and let the opening track fill the room.
"Good evening, night owls. You're tuned to Wavelength, and I'm your ghost for the next four hours."
The call light blinked almost immediately — a man from somewhere out past the highway, voice thick with static.
He wanted a song from 1973, one nobody had requested since the station flipped formats.
I dug through the crate, found a vinyl sleeve cracked at the corner, and dropped the needle.
Music spilled out, warm and imperfect, exactly the way memory should sound.
By two a.m., the calls grew stranger: a woman asking for a lullaby in a language she couldn't name,
a teenager playing pranks with a friend, both pretending to be someone else,
and an old man who just wanted to hear his voice on the radio one more time before bed.
I kept the playlist loose, letting the requests thread together like a conversation between strangers who would never meet.
Outside the booth, the city slept, indifferent to the invisible web of voices connecting bedroom to bedroom.
At dawn, the morning show producer knocked on the glass, already scrolling through her phone, already impatient.
I handed her the logs with a nod, packed my headphones into their battered case,
and walked out into a morning that had no idea what the night had sounded like.
